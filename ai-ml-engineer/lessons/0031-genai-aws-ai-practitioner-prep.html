Generative AI concepts & AWS AI Practitioner exam prep

Lesson 28 showed you how a transformer works under the hood — attention, next-token prediction, all of it. "Generative AI" is what people call the industry built on top of that architecture: models that generate new text, images, code, or audio instead of just classifying or predicting a number. This lesson gives you the vocabulary that industry uses every day, then walks you through exactly what the AWS Certified AI Practitioner exam expects you to know — confirmed against AWS's own certification page and exam guide, not guessed.

Part 1 — Generative AI vocabulary

These five terms show up constantly in job postings, AWS documentation, and the exam itself. Each one builds on what you already learned in Lesson 28.

Foundation model

A foundation model is a large model — usually a transformer, exactly like the one in Lesson 28 — pre-trained on a huge, general dataset, then adapted to many different downstream tasks. "Foundation" is the key word: it's a base you build on, not a model built for one narrow job. GPT, Claude, and Amazon Titan are all foundation models. The same pre-trained model can answer questions, summarize documents, write code, or hold a conversation, depending on how you use it.

Prompting

Prompting is giving the model instructions in plain text to get the output you want — no retraining involved. "Summarize this in 3 bullet points" or "Write this email in a formal tone" are prompts. This is the cheapest and fastest way to steer a foundation model, and it's why so much of "using AI" today is really just learning to write clear instructions.

Fine-tuning

Fine-tuning means taking a pre-trained foundation model and training it further on your own specific data, so it gets better at your particular task or adopts your tone/format. It's more expensive and slower than prompting — you're updating the model's internal parameters, not just asking it nicely — so it's usually a later step, only used when prompting alone isn't good enough.

RAG — Retrieval-Augmented Generation

RAG means giving the model relevant documents or data at request time, so it can answer using information it was never trained on — your company's internal wiki, last week's support tickets, today's stock price. The model retrieves the relevant pieces first, then generates its answer using both the question and those retrieved documents.

Simple example: a foundation model without RAG is like a very well-read person answering purely from memory — impressive, but their knowledge has a cutoff date and doesn't include your private files. RAG is the same person now allowed to open a folder of your documents before answering. If you've used an AI chat assistant that can search your files or a knowledge base before responding, that's RAG — it's also the core pattern behind most "AI agents" you may have already seen at work, which look things up before they act.

Responsible AI

Foundation models inherit patterns — including bad ones — from the data they were trained on. Bias means the model's outputs can unfairly favor or disfavor certain groups because that pattern existed in the training data. Hallucination means the model can state something completely wrong with total confidence — it has no built-in way to say "I don't know," so it generates a plausible-sounding answer even when it isn't true. Neither of these is a bug you can fully "fix" — they're properties of how these models work, which is exactly why responsible AI practices (testing outputs, human review, citing sources, RAG to ground answers in real documents) are a required skill, not an optional extra. This is one of the five domains on the exam you're about to prep for.

Try it — before looking below

Q1. Your team wants a chatbot that can answer questions using your company's internal policy documents, which the model was never trained on. Do you reach for prompting, fine-tuning, or RAG — and why?

RAG. The model needs access to specific, current documents it wasn't trained on — RAG retrieves the relevant policy text at request time and hands it to the model along with the question. Fine-tuning would be slower, more expensive, and doesn't naturally keep up when the policies change; prompting alone can't give the model information it has never seen.

Q2. A foundation model gives you a confident, detailed answer that turns out to be completely false. What is this called, and why does it happen?

A hallucination. The model generates the next most plausible-sounding tokens (Lesson 28) — it has no built-in mechanism to check "is this actually true" or say "I don't know," so a fluent, confident-sounding answer can still be factually wrong.

Part 2 — AWS Certified AI Practitioner (AIF-C01) exam prep

Lesson 1 already flagged this cert as your fastest resume-visible badge, slotted around Phase I. Here's the exam confirmed directly against AWS's official certification page and exam guide (aws.amazon.com and docs.aws.amazon.com) — no guessing.

What it actually tests — the 5 domains

This is the exam guide's official content outline, with the exact weightings AWS publishes:

DomainWeight
1. Fundamentals of AI and ML20%
2. Fundamentals of Generative AI24%
3. Applications of Foundation Models28%
4. Guidelines for Responsible AI14%
5. Security, Compliance, and Governance for AI Solutions14%

Notice domains 2 and 3 together are over half the exam (52%) — that's exactly the vocabulary from Part 1 above: foundation models, prompting, fine-tuning, RAG, and how to apply them to business problems on AWS (mainly through Amazon Bedrock and SageMaker AI). Domain 4, responsible AI, is the bias/hallucination material you just read. The exam explicitly does not expect you to code, build ML pipelines, or do hyperparameter tuning — it tests whether you understand and can apply these concepts, not whether you can build the models yourself.

Q3. Roughly how many of the 65 exam questions actually count toward your score, and why does that number matter when you're pacing yourself in the 90 minutes?

50 questions are scored; the other 15 are unscored questions AWS is testing for future exams, mixed in without being labeled. It doesn't change your pacing strategy — since you can't tell which is which, you have to treat every one of the 65 as if it counts, giving each one fair attention across the 90 minutes.

Q4. Which two exam domains together make up more than half the exam, and what topic from Part 1 of this lesson do they map to?

Domain 2 (Fundamentals of GenAI, 24%) and Domain 3 (Applications of Foundation Models, 28%) — together 52%. They map directly to the vocabulary in Part 1: foundation models, prompting, fine-tuning, and RAG, plus applying them to real business use cases on AWS.
