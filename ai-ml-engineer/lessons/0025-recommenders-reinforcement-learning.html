Recommender systems & intro to reinforcement learning

This lesson is a survey, not deep hands-on. Two big ideas, in plain terms: how apps like Netflix and Spotify decide what to show you next (recommender systems), and how an AI can learn by trial and error instead of from labeled examples (reinforcement learning).

Recommender systems

A recommender system's job is simple: given what you've liked or watched or bought before, guess what you'll want next. There are two main approaches.

Collaborative filtering

Collaborative filtering looks at other people, not the item itself. The logic: "people who liked what you liked also liked X, so you'll probably like X too." It never needs to understand what a movie is about — it just needs a big table of who liked what.

Simple example: Netflix notices that most people who rated Movie A highly also rated Movie B highly. You just rated Movie A highly. Netflix recommends Movie B — even though it never looked at what either movie is about.

Content-based filtering

Content-based filtering looks at the item's features — genre, artist, actors, keywords — and recommends things similar to what you already liked. It doesn't need other users at all, just a description of each item and your own history.

Simple example: Spotify sees you liked several acoustic indie songs. It looks for other songs tagged with similar features (tempo, instruments, genre) and recommends those — based purely on the songs' properties, not on what other listeners did.

In practice, big platforms usually blend both approaches — collaborative filtering is powerful once you have lots of user data, content-based filtering works even for a brand-new item nobody has rated yet.

ApproachUsesWeak point
Collaborative filteringPatterns across many usersStruggles with new items nobody has rated yet
Content-based filteringFeatures of the item itselfTends to recommend "more of the same," less discovery

Reinforcement learning (RL)

Every model so far in this course has learned from a fixed, labeled dataset. Reinforcement learning is different: there's no dataset of "correct answers." Instead, an agent takes actions inside an environment, and gets a reward (a number — good or bad) after each action. Over many, many attempts, the agent learns which actions tend to lead to more reward. No math needed here — just the loop: act, observe the result, get a reward, adjust, repeat.

Simple example: a game-playing agent learning to play a simple game like Pong starts out moving randomly. Sometimes it happens to hit the ball and scores a point (positive reward); sometimes it misses (no reward, or a penalty). It doesn't get told the "right" move — it just tries things, notices which actions came before a reward, and slowly starts favoring those actions. After enough games, it plays well, without a human ever writing "if ball is here, move paddle there."

The same loop shows up in robots learning to walk (reward = staying upright and moving forward), and in RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback), which is part of how chatbots like ChatGPT get fine-tuned to give better responses — human preferences act as the reward signal.

PieceWhat it means
AgentThe learner/decision-maker (the game AI, the robot, the model)
EnvironmentThe world it acts in (the game, the physical robot's surroundings)
ActionA choice the agent makes (move paddle up, take a step forward)
RewardA number telling the agent how good that action turned out to be

Try it — classify these

For each short scenario, decide: collaborative filtering, content-based filtering, or reinforcement learning?

Q1. A music app recommends a new song because it has a similar tempo, key, and genre tags to songs you've saved before.

Content-based filtering — it's matching item features, not other users' behavior.

Q2. A shopping site recommends a product because "customers who bought this also bought that."

Collaborative filtering — it's based on patterns across many users, not the product's own features.

Q3. A robot tries different leg movements, falls over a few times, and gradually learns a walking pattern that keeps it upright longest.

Reinforcement learning — trial and error, guided by a reward signal (staying upright), no labeled dataset of "correct" movements.

Recap
  • Collaborative filtering: recommend based on what similar users liked.
  • Content-based filtering: recommend based on item features similar to what you liked before.
  • Reinforcement learning: an agent learns by taking actions, getting rewards, and adjusting — trial and error, no labeled dataset.
  • Real systems often mix approaches — e.g. collaborative filtering plus content-based to handle new items.
