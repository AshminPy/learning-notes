Ansible / Zero to Expert — Lesson 1 of 36

Your roadmap: zero to Ansible expert

You want to be able to open a real fleet of servers, describe the configuration they need in code, and push that configuration out with confidence — repeatably, safely, on a real team. You currently know zero Ansible. That gap closes in stages, not in one leap. This lesson is the map — read it once now, come back to it whenever you feel lost.

The one thing to remember: the goal of this course is production automation confidence, not a certificate. The Red Hat Certified Specialist in Developing Automation with Ansible Automation Platform (EX374) shows up later as a checkpoint — a way to prove the fundamentals are solid — but passing it is not the finish line. Being able to write, review, and safely operate Ansible automation against real hosts is the finish line.

Why this order

Every phase is a prerequisite for the one after it — skipping ahead just means backfilling later, so trust the sequence:

  • You can't learn the core playbook language (Phase C) before you've run a playbook at all (Phase B). Variables, facts, and conditionals only make sense once you've watched a simple playbook connect to a host over SSH and change something idempotently. Idempotency in particular — the single most important concept in this whole course — has to be felt once before it can be reasoned about.
  • You can't organize automation into roles (Phase E) before you understand tasks, variables, and templates (Phases C-D). A role is just a directory convention for reusable tasks, variables, handlers, and templates. If you don't know what those pieces are individually, a role's folder structure is just memorized boilerplate.
  • You can't manage inventory and variables at scale (Phase F) before you understand basic inventory and variables (Phase B-C). Dynamic inventory and variable precedence are the scaled-up version of concepts you first meet as a flat inventory file and a simple vars: block. Learn the small version first.
  • You can't reason about advanced techniques like delegation and Vault (Phase G) before you've built and organized real playbooks and roles (Phases B-E). Error handling, tags, and secrets management are refinements to automation you already know how to write — not a starting point.
  • You can't test automation meaningfully (Phase H) before there's automation worth testing (Phases B-G). ansible-lint and Molecule exist to catch mistakes in playbooks and roles — you need real playbooks and roles first, and enough scar tissue to know what a mistake even looks like.
  • You can't reason about production concerns (Phase I) before you've seen the full lifecycle (Phases B-H). Execution environments, AWX, and CI/CD pipelines are what experienced teams add once the underlying automation is already solid — bolting them on earlier just adds ceremony with nothing behind it yet.
  • The certification (Phase J) comes at the end, not the start. EX374 is a 4-hour, hands-on, performance-based lab exam covering Git workflows, inventory/variable management, task execution, filters, delegation, collections, execution environments, and automation controller — content spanning nearly every phase above. Studying for it before Phase I would mean attempting lab tasks with no hands-on foundation underneath them.

The certification — and why it's a milestone, not the goal

Checked directly against Red Hat's own exam page (2026-07-30) — here's what's actually true about the Red Hat Certified Specialist in Developing Automation with Ansible Automation Platform (EX374) right now:

  • Exam code: EX374
  • Format: hands-on, practical, performance-based — Red Hat's own wording: "requires you to undertake real-world tasks." This is not a multiple-choice exam.
  • Duration: 4 hours, one section
  • Tests: Red Hat Ansible Automation Platform 2.5
  • Prerequisites: completing AU374 (Developing Advanced Automation) or RH294 (Red Hat Linux Automation with Ansible) is recommended — or comparable hands-on experience. Not a hard gate, but the exam assumes that level of practical fluency.
  • During the exam: no internet access, no external documentation — only the product documentation shipped with the exam environment
  • Results: reported within 3 business days
  • Counts toward: Red Hat Certified Architect (RHCA)

This is the important honest bit: EX374 is a lab exam, not a knowledge-recall test. You are graded on whether real automation you write, against real hosts, under time pressure, actually works — not on whether you can recognize the right answer in a list of four. Reading this course builds the mental models and the syntax fluency, but it cannot substitute for hands-on repetition on real hosts (a local VM, a set of containers, whatever you have access to). If you only ever read the lessons and never type the commands yourself, you will not be ready for this exam — and more importantly, you won't be ready for a real production incident either. That's why every phase in this course pairs concepts with something you actually run.

Try it — before looking below

Q1. Why can't you learn roles (Phase E) before you understand tasks, variables, and templates (Phases C-D)?

A role is just a directory convention for organizing tasks, variables, handlers, and templates you already know how to write individually. Without understanding those pieces first, a role's folder structure is just memorized boilerplate — you won't know what to put where or why the convention helps.

Q2. Is the EX374 exam multiple choice? How long is it, and what's the recommended prerequisite?

No — it's a hands-on, practical, performance-based exam where you complete real tasks against real systems, not a multiple-choice test. It's 4 hours, one section, testing Ansible Automation Platform 2.5. Recommended prerequisite: AU374 or RH294, or comparable hands-on experience.
