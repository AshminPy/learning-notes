GCP PCA — Lesson 24 of 25

Reliability in production

Lesson 12 covered observability — seeing what's happening. This lesson is the other half of Section 6: proving, on purpose and before it matters, that the system actually holds up.

Simple example: a fire drill isn't about the fire. It's finding out the exit door sticks before there's smoke in the building. Chaos engineering, penetration testing, and load testing are all the same idea — break it on purpose, on your schedule, not the internet's.

Deployment & release management

Beyond just running Cloud Deploy (Lesson 17): release management means every release is versioned, rollback is a rehearsed action not a hope, and a rollout can be paused or reversed mid-way (canary, blue/green) without customers ever needing to know something went wrong.

Support of deployed solutions & quality control

An SLO (Service Level Objective — e.g. "99.9% of requests succeed") and its error budget (the allowed 0.1% failure) turn "is it reliable enough" from a feeling into a number. Quality control is the pre-release gate that protects that budget: code review, automated test coverage, and canary analysis (comparing the new version's real metrics against the old one before a full rollout) all exist to catch a budget-burning regression before 100% of traffic sees it.

Exam trap: "we have 100% uptime as a goal" is not a real SLO — an SLO always has a stated target below 100% and a matching error budget. A scenario demanding zero failures with no budget mentioned is testing whether you catch that unrealistic framing, not endorse it.

Reliability in production — proving it on purpose

  • Chaos engineering — deliberately injecting failure (kill a Pod, cut a dependency) in a controlled way to verify the system actually degrades the way you assumed it would, instead of finding out during a real outage.
  • Penetration testing — authorized, deliberate attack simulation against your own systems to find real vulnerabilities before an attacker does. Google's Acceptable Use Policy permits testing your own GCP projects without prior notification to Google — but scope and authorization within your own org still matter.
  • Load testing — driving realistic (or peak-projected) traffic at a system before launch to find the actual breaking point, instead of discovering it live during a traffic spike.

All three share the same shape: find the failure on your terms, with a rollback plan ready, instead of live in production with customers watching.

Try it — before looking below

Q1. A team says their reliability target is "zero downtime, ever." What's the architect's response, and why?

Push back and define a real SLO with an explicit error budget. "Zero, ever" isn't achievable or measurable — every real reliability target has a stated threshold below 100%, which is exactly what makes it possible to know whether you're meeting it.

Quiz — Lesson 24

Q1. What's the actual purpose of chaos engineering?

A. It's a controlled, deliberate way to validate assumptions about failure behavior — not randomness for its own sake.

Q2. What does an error budget actually represent?

A. If the SLO is 99.9%, the error budget is the 0.1% of allowed failure — spend it on risk (faster releases) or save it, but it's a real, trackable quantity, not a vague goal.

Recap
  • An SLO always has a target below 100%, with an explicit error budget — "zero downtime ever" isn't a real target.
  • Chaos engineering, penetration testing, and load testing all find failure on your terms, before it happens for real.
  • Canary analysis compares new-version metrics against old before a full rollout — a quality gate, not just a deploy mechanism.
