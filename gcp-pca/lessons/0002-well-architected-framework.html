Well-Architected Framework

Most exam questions have 2 or more answers that are technically correct. The Well-Architected Framework is the lens that picks the "best" one.

Simple example: think of it like a restaurant menu with a dietary filter turned on. Every dish (answer) is edible (technically valid) — but only one fits the filter (the pillar) the scenario is hinting at.

The 6 pillars

PillarWhat it's really asking
Operational ExcellenceLow ongoing ops burden on your team?
Security, Privacy & ComplianceLeast privilege, encryption, data residency?
ReliabilitySurvives a zone/region failure at the stated SLA?
Cost OptimizationBusiness value per dollar, not just "cheapest"
Performance OptimizationLatency/throughput matched to the workload?
SustainabilityRegion/resource choice minimizes carbon footprint?

Exam trap: a technically correct answer can still be wrong — it just optimizes the wrong pillar for the scenario.

Naming success before you design

Before any pillar trade-off, a real design starts with how success gets measured — a KPI (e.g. "99.9% of requests under 200ms") or an ROI target (e.g. "cut infra spend 20% within a year"). A scenario that states one of these isn't decoration — it's telling you which pillar to weight heaviest, before you even reach the elimination algorithm below.

Elimination algorithm

  1. Underline the constraint keywords ("minimal ops," "strict compliance," "lowest cost") — they name the pillar.
  2. Eliminate options that violate that pillar, even if they work technically.
  3. Eliminate options solving the wrong problem (e.g. only addressing cost when the question is about consistency).
  4. Between what's left, default to less custom engineering — unless the scenario explicitly demands the extra control.

Try it

Scenario: a small ops team needs the cheapest storage for product images, with global low-latency reads. Option A: Cloud Storage multi-region bucket. Option B: a self-managed NFS server on a VM. Which do you eliminate first?

B. "Small ops team" is the constraint keyword — self-managing an NFS server violates Operational Excellence. Cloud Storage is managed and built for exactly this.

Quiz — Lesson 2

Q1. A question hinges on "least privilege" and "customer-managed encryption keys." Which pillar is being tested?

A. Least privilege and key management are Security/Privacy/Compliance signals, not cost, performance, or sustainability.

Q2. Two answers are technically correct. One means your team owns ongoing patching, and there's a strict budget. What do you eliminate?

A. Step 4 of the algorithm: default to less ops burden — that's also cheaper once you count engineer-hours, which is what "strict budget" is really asking.

Q3. No budget or compliance constraint is stated. One option is self-managed, one is managed. What's the default move?

A. Absent an explicit reason to self-manage, the Operational Excellence pillar favors the managed service by default.

Q4. How many Well-Architected pillars does Google define today?

A. Six — Sustainability was added later, so older material listing five is out of date.

Recap
  • 6 pillars: Operational Excellence, Security, Reliability, Cost, Performance, Sustainability.
