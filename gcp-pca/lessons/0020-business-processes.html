GCP PCA — Lesson 20 of 24

Business processes

No GCP product names in this lesson — this is the half of Section 4 that tests whether you can recognize a people/organization problem when a scenario disguises it as a technical one.

Simple example: a migration stalls. The tempting answer is always "wrong tool." Half the time the real answer is "wrong stakeholder never signed off" or "the team doesn't have the skills yet" — and no service choice fixes either of those.

Stakeholder management

Business and technical stakeholders often want different things from the same project (speed vs. cost vs. risk). The architect's job includes surfacing that conflict explicitly, not picking a side unasked. On the exam: if a scenario names two people with conflicting goals, the "best" answer usually addresses both goals' underlying concern, not just the more technical-sounding one.

Change management

Getting an organization to actually adopt a new process or platform — different from a Kubernetes "rolling update." A technically perfect migration that nobody uses because the team wasn't looped in early is a change-management failure, not a technical one.

Team assessment / skills readiness

Before recommending an architecture, whether the team can actually run it matters. Recommending GKE + a service mesh to a team with zero Kubernetes experience, with no training plan, is a real exam trap — technically correct, organizationally set up to fail.

Exam trap: the "best" architecture on paper isn't the best answer if the scenario explicitly says the team lacks the skills to operate it and there's no mention of a training plan. Watch for a simpler answer that matches the team's actual readiness.

Decision-making processes & customer success

Decision-making processes — who actually has authority to approve a design, and how disagreements get resolved, defined before you need it, not during an incident. Customer success management — for a solution built for an external customer, ensuring they actually adopt and get value from it, not just that it was delivered and works.

Business continuity vs. disaster recovery

DR (Lesson 13) is about systems: RPO, RTO, backup and failover. Business continuity is broader — can the business keep operating at all, including the people and processes, not just the IT systems. A DR plan can be flawless and the business still fails to continue if there's no plan for how staff work during the outage.

Try it — before looking below

Q1. A scenario says a proposed migration is technically sound, but the ops team has never touched the target platform and no training is mentioned. What's the most likely "best answer" theme?

Something addressing team readiness — a phased rollout, a training plan, or a simpler architecture the team can actually operate. Not a more advanced technical option; the gap in the scenario is organizational, not technical.

Quiz — Lesson 20

Q1. What's the key difference between disaster recovery and business continuity?

A. A perfect technical failover doesn't guarantee the business itself keeps functioning — BC is the broader, organizational version of the same question.

Q2. A migration is complete and passes every technical test, but adoption is near zero six months later. What process most likely failed?

A. Zero adoption despite working tech is a classic change-management failure, not a technical one.

Recap
  • A stalled or unadopted project is often a stakeholder/change-management problem, not a wrong-service problem.
  • Team skills readiness can make the "best" architecture the wrong answer if there's no plan to build those skills.
  • Business continuity is broader than disaster recovery — it includes people and process, not just systems.
