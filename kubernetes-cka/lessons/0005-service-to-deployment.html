Service → Deployment

This is a practical lesson, not a new concept — it applies Lessons 2-4 together. You already wired a Service to a bare Pod (Lesson 3) and built a Deployment (Lesson 4). Wire them together, and see the one thing that's easy to get wrong: where a Deployment's Pod labels actually live.

Deployment
  spec.template.metadata.labels: app=web   ← a Service selector matches THIS
      │
      ▼
Pod   label: app=web
      ▲
      │  (selector: app=web)
Service

A Service has no idea a Deployment exists — it never reads spec.selector.matchLabels (that's the Deployment's own bookkeeping) or the Deployment's top-level metadata.labels (that's the Deployment object's own labels, not its Pods'). It only ever matches Pod labels, which live at spec.template.metadata.labels inside the Deployment.

Try it

Q1. Does a Service care whether its matched Pods came from a Deployment, a ReplicaSet, or were created bare?

No. A Service only matches labels — nothing in its spec references who or what created a Pod.

Q2. During a rolling update, which Pods does a Service send traffic to — old, new, or both?

Both — any Pod matching the selector and currently Ready, regardless of which ReplicaSet (old or new) owns it.

Now do it: reuse Lesson 4's Deployment, write a ClusterIP Service selecting app: web, apply both, and confirm with kubectl get endpoints that it lists all 3 Pod IPs. Try before revealing.

Reveal the reference answer

apiVersion: v1
kind: Service
metadata:
  name: web-svc
spec:
  selector:
    app: web
  ports:
  - name: http
    port: 80
    targetPort: 80

A controlled failure

Predict, then run: start a rolling update to a slow-starting image, and watch endpoints mid-flight.

kubectl set image deployment/web web=nginx:1.29
kubectl get endpoints web-svc --watch

Observe: the endpoint count doesn't drop to 0 and jump back — it grows and shrinks gradually as new Pods become Ready and old ones terminate. That's the "both, if Ready" answer above, made visible in real time. Stop watching with Ctrl-C when it settles at 3.

Production note: this is exactly why a Deployment's readiness probe (Lesson 11) matters for zero-downtime rollouts — a new Pod that's Running but not yet Ready gets zero traffic from the Service, so a slow-starting app doesn't get real requests before it can handle them.

Exam & interview notes

Combines Workloads & Scheduling (15%) and Services & Networking (20%) — a very common live-troubleshooting combination on the exam.

Interview Q: "Your Service selector is app: web but traffic still fails after deploying. Where do you check first?" A: The Deployment's spec.template.metadata.labels, not its top-level metadata.labels or spec.selector.matchLabels — that's the field a Service actually reads.

Recap
  • A Service matches Pod labels only — it has no concept of Deployments, ReplicaSets, or ownership.
  • A Deployment's Pod labels live at spec.template.metadata.labels — that's the field a Service selector must match.
  • A Service routes to any matching, Ready Pod — old and new can both receive traffic mid-rollout.

Confidence check — answer without looking back:

  • Why doesn't a Service need to know a Deployment exists at all?
  • When could a rolling update send traffic to a broken new Pod? (Hint: think about probes.)
  • What happens to endpoints if a Deployment's template labels change mid-rollout?
  • How would you verify traffic is reaching both old and new Pods during a rollout?
