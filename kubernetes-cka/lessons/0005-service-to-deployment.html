Kubernetes / CKA — Lesson 5 of 17

Service → Deployment

Lesson 3 wired a Service to a bare Pod. Lesson 4 built a Deployment. This lesson doesn't add a new mechanism — it proves the one you already know still works, unchanged, one layer up.

Tied to your mission: a lot of CKA troubleshooting scenarios are "the Service isn't sending traffic to my Deployment's Pods." If you understand this lesson, that class of bug stops being mysterious — it's always a label mismatch, somewhere.

The mental model

A Service does exactly one thing to decide where traffic goes: it looks at spec.selector, and it matches that against the labels on running Pods. That's the whole algorithm. It doesn't look at kind, it doesn't ask "who owns this Pod," it doesn't know a Deployment or ReplicaSet exists. Kubernetes itself is explicit about this — Services exist so you don't have to change how your app is discovered no matter how the Pods behind it were created.

So when a Deployment is in the picture, the only thing that changes is where the labels are written. A bare Pod has its labels at metadata.labels. A Deployment's Pods get their labels from spec.template.metadata.labels — the "template" is the Pod spec the Deployment stamps out, and that template has its own metadata, separate from the Deployment's own metadata.labels up top.

apiVersion: apps/v1
kind: Deployment
metadata:
  name: nginx-deployment
  labels:            # <- labels on the Deployment object itself. Service never reads this.
    app: nginx
spec:
  replicas: 3
  selector:
    matchLabels:
      app: nginx      # <- how the Deployment finds ITS OWN Pods
  template:
    metadata:
      labels:
        app: nginx    # <- labels on the PODS. This is what a Service selector matches.
    spec:
      containers:
        - name: nginx
          image: nginx:1.14.2

The "aha": a Service selecting a Deployment's Pods is the identical mechanism as Lesson 3's Service-to-Pod — selector labels matches Pod labels. Nothing Deployment-specific exists in the Service spec. If you can wire a Service to one Pod, you already know how to wire it to a Deployment; you just point the selector at spec.template.metadata.labels instead of a Pod's own metadata.labels.

One more piece worth having now, even though it's not the focus until Lesson 11: during a rolling update, a Service doesn't pause or specially handle the transition. It keeps doing the same thing it always does — send traffic to whatever Pods currently match the selector and are Ready. Old and new Pods both carry app: nginx during a rollout (only an internal pod-template-hash label tells them apart), so the Service happily routes to both while they coexist. If a new Pod isn't Ready yet, the Service simply doesn't send it traffic — it's excluded from the endpoint list until its readiness probe passes. That's why readiness matters so much operationally: it's the only thing standing between "rollout in progress" and "users hitting a Pod that can't serve requests yet." More on defining that in Lesson 11.

Try it first — before reading on

Q1. Does a Service care whether the Pods it targets were created by a Deployment, a ReplicaSet, or as bare Pods? Why or why not?

No. A Service only reads spec.selector and matches it against Pod labels at request time. It has no field referencing a Deployment, ReplicaSet, or owner at all — whatever created the Pod is irrelevant to the Service.

Q2. In a Deployment manifest, where exactly do the Pod labels live that a Service's selector needs to match?

spec.template.metadata.labels — not the Deployment's own top-level metadata.labels, and not spec.selector.matchLabels (that one is the Deployment's own selector for finding its Pods, not the Service's).

Q3. During a rolling update, which Pods does a Service send traffic to, and what decides whether a given Pod is eligible?

Any Pod that currently matches the Service's label selector and is Ready. Old and new-version Pods both match the selector during a rollout, so both can receive traffic — a Pod only drops out of the Service's endpoints if its readiness probe is failing (or it has no readiness probe configured and isn't yet reporting ready).

Now build it — in your terminal, not here

Write a ClusterIP Service by hand that selects the Pods from the Deployment you built in Lesson 4. Requirements only — no manifest here:

  • kind: Service
  • a selector that matches the labels in your Deployment's spec.template.metadata.labels — not the Deployment's own metadata.labels, and not its name
  • no type field needed — ClusterIP is the default, but you can write it explicitly if you want to be sure
  • a ports entry: the port the Service listens on, and targetPort matching the container port your Deployment's Pods actually expose

Write it, then check it field-by-field against the reference below.

Reveal the reference answer

apiVersion: v1
kind: Service
metadata:
  name: nginx-service
spec:
  selector:
    app: nginx
  ports:
    - protocol: TCP
      port: 80
      targetPort: 80

Notice what's absent: nothing here names the Deployment. kubectl get endpoints nginx-service would show you the actual Pod IPs it resolved to — proof the Service is working off labels, not names.

Recap
  • A Service matches spec.selector against Pod labels — full stop. It has no idea a Deployment exists.
  • A Deployment's Pod labels live at spec.template.metadata.labels, distinct from the Deployment's own metadata.labels and from spec.selector.matchLabels.
  • During a rolling update, the Service keeps routing to any matching Pod that is Ready — old and new Pods can both receive traffic mid-rollout. Readiness is what makes this safe (Lesson 11).
