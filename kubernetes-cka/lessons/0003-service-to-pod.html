Service → Pod (ClusterIP)

Lesson 1 established that Pods get replaced, usually with a new IP. That's a problem the moment something else needs to reach a Pod reliably — a frontend calling a backend, a job calling an API. Hardcode a Pod IP and it's stale within hours. A Service is Kubernetes's answer: a stable address that keeps finding the right Pods, live, no matter how many times they're replaced.

Mental model: a Service is a company's front-desk phone number, not a direct extension. Staff (Pods) come and go, move desks, quit — the front-desk number never changes, but who answers does, automatically, based on who currently has the job (the label). Call it right after someone's replaced and you still get through, with zero awareness anything changed.

Where this fits

Cluster └ Node └ Pod └ Labels
                          └ Service       ← you are here (reads labels, doesn't own them)

A Service doesn't create or manage Pods — it only watches labels (Lesson 2) and routes to whatever currently matches. Lesson 4's Deployment sits at the same level, doing the opposite job: creating and replacing Pods. Together they're the two most common consumers of the label mechanism you already know.

What's inside a Service manifest

apiVersion: v1
kind: Service
metadata:
  name: web-svc
spec:
  type: ClusterIP
  selector:
    app: web
  ports:
  - name: http
    port: 80
    targetPort: 80
  • spec.type — ClusterIP is the default: reachable only inside the cluster
  • spec.selector — must exactly match a Pod's metadata.labels (Lesson 2's mechanism, unchanged), or the Service finds nothing
  • port — what clients dial; targetPort — what the container actually listens on

Why it's built this way: since Lesson 1 made Pods disposable and Lesson 2 made "which Pods" a live label query instead of a fixed list, a Service is the natural next piece — it's just a selector query (like kubectl get pods -l) that also gets a stable IP and DNS name attached to it. Nothing new conceptually, just wired into the network.

No selector match, no traffic — the Service exists and reports healthy either way, silently. Inside the cluster, reach it by DNS: <service>.<namespace>.svc.cluster.local.

Try it

Q1. You create a Service and see no errors. What command tells you whether it actually found any Pods?

kubectl get endpoints <service-name> — an empty list means the selector matched nothing.

Now write your own: a ClusterIP Service for your Lesson 2 Pod, by hand — selector matching its labels, port forwarding to its container port. Try before revealing.

Reveal the reference answer

apiVersion: v1
kind: Service
metadata:
  name: web-svc
spec:
  type: ClusterIP
  selector:
    app: web
  ports:
  - name: http
    port: 80
    targetPort: 80

Common mistake: mixing up port and targetPort — get them backward and you get a Service with a full, correct-looking endpoint list that still refuses every connection.

A controlled failure

kubectl run web --image=nginx:1.29 --labels=app=web
kubectl expose pod web --port=80 --name=web-svc
kubectl patch service web-svc -p '{"spec":{"selector":{"app":"webapp"}}}'

Predict first: what will kubectl get endpoints web-svc show after that patch? Then run it. Observe: <none> — the Service still looks completely healthy in kubectl get svc, it just found nothing. Restore it:

kubectl patch service web-svc -p '{"spec":{"selector":{"app":"web"}}}'
kubectl get endpoints web-svc

Production best practices

Production note: always call other services by DNS name (web-svc or the full web-svc.default.svc.cluster.local), never by Pod IP or Service ClusterIP — the name is the only part guaranteed stable. Named ports (name: http, as above) are the real-world default too, not just numbers, because a Service can expose more than one port and names disambiguate them.

Exam & interview notes

Interview Q: "A Service and its Pods both look healthy, but traffic fails. What's your first check?" A: kubectl get endpoints <service> — a clean kubectl apply proves nothing about whether the selector actually matched anything.

Recap
  • A Service = a stable IP + DNS name that continuously matches its selector against Pod labels — the same mechanism from Lesson 2, wired into the network.
  • port is what clients dial; targetPort is what the container listens on.
  • A selector mismatch is silent — the Service reports healthy either way. kubectl get endpoints is the real check.
